# Matty Groves / Little Musgrave

---

## An attempt at a telling

It was a Sunday. A high day, or a holiday, and young Matty Groves, in some tales, Little Musgrave, in others, was sat near the back, watching the ladies, looking at the girls, rather than attending to his prayer book.

Fairest among them was Lord Bernard's wife, and as he watched her leave the church, he was sure he saw her pass a glance at him.

And again the next week, only this time, with a smile. And again the week after. Which he took as an opening...

The story — the song — doesn't say how he managed it, what with her being lady, and he, a low-born young labouring lad, but she offered him a lift home in her carriage one week, and took the lead — "I've seen you, around, I've seen you, watching me", and he blushed, and she put her hand on his leg beside her...

"I have a bower, at Bucklesfordbury. Meet me there, tonight, we will spend the night together..."

And Little Musgrave, "oh, I will, my Lady, I will, I will", and the date was set.

But a page boy riding at the back of the carriage had heard the invitation, and though he was his lady's page, he was also his master's man, and he passed the message on.

"If what you tell me is true, I will reward you for it... but if you're telling me lies, I'll see you hang for it", and Lord Barnard, and set off Bucklesfordbury himself.

And as he rode through the forest, he sounded his hunting horn, because this was a much a hunt as any ride he had ever been on.

Wrapped in his mistress's arms, young Musgrave — "is that a hunting horn? My lady, perhaps I should take my leave..", but she wrapped her arms round him again, "don't be silly, Little Musgrave, it just a shepherd's boy, taking his sheep to to the fold," and she kissed again, took him under the covers again.

Outside the bower, Lord Barnard dismounts, takes the first of three keys from his belt. Unlocks the door into the bower. The door at the bottom of the stairs. Quietly, ever so quietly, the door at the top — the door to his wife's chamber.

Quietly, crosses the room, throws back the bed cover.

"So, little Musgrave, how are you enjoying MY WIFE?"

And Little Musgrave — what's the right answer in such a situation? — "erm, very much?"

"Get out of that bed, and put some clothes on. I won't have it said I've killed a naked man."

"And you shall have my best sword, again so no-one can say I didn't give you a fair chance."

And Matty Groves dressed himself, and took the sword, and struck the first blow. But Lord Barnard took the second, and Matty Groves would not strike a blow ever again.

And Lord Barnard's wife let out a cry, and said a prayer for young Matty Groves. And Lord Barnard swung his sword again, and his wife ebbed away with that of her lover.

And Lord Barnard had a grave dug, threw Matty Groves in, and his wife on top.

And that is the end of the tale of Matty Groves, or as he is also known, Little Musgrave.

In the establishment of a feudal baron, every thing wore a military aspect; he was a warrior by profession ; every man attached to him, particularly those employed about his person, was a soldier ; and his little foot-page was very appropriately equipped in the light accoutrements of an archer. His bow, in the old ballad, seems as inseparable from his character as the bow of Cupid or of Apollo, or the caduceus of his celestial prototype Mercury. This bow, which he carried unbent, he seems to have bent when he had occasion to swim, in order that he might the more easily carry it in his teeth, to prevent the string from being injured, by getting wet At other times, he availed himself of its length and elasticity in the brent, or straight state, and used it (as hunters do a leaping pole) in vaulting ever the wall of the outer court of a castle, when his business would not admit of the tedious formality of blowing a horn, or ringing a. bell, and holding a long parley with the porter at the gate, before he could gain admission. This at least, appears to the editor to be the meaning of these passages in the old ballads.
